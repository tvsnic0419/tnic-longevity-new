# Sirtuin Atlas — evidence notes

Last reviewed: 2026-08-26

This source note supports the interactive `/sirtuin-atlas` module. It intentionally separates four claims that are often collapsed into the phrase **sirtuin activator**:

1. **Direct activation** — evidence that a molecule increases catalytic activity of a sirtuin isoform.
2. **NAD+ support** — raises/provides the required NAD+ cosubstrate; family-wide substrate support, not selective activation.
3. **Expression / signaling** — changes sirtuin abundance or pathway signaling without proving direct catalytic activation.
4. **Uncertain** — plausible or marketed as a sirtuin intervention, but target engagement is not established.

## High-confidence conclusions

- No OTC supplement is proven to selectively activate SIRT1 through SIRT7 in humans.
- NR and NMN are best treated as NAD+ restoration strategies, not seven-way direct agonists.
- Resveratrol has substrate-dependent SIRT1 allosteric biology, but human SIRT1 results are mixed; a 2025 meta-analysis of 11 RCTs found no significant overall increase in SIRT1 gene expression, protein expression, or serum levels.
- SRT2104 has the strongest human-tested selective SIRT1 pharmacology in this atlas, but it is a research drug rather than an approved anti-aging therapy.
- Honokiol is a compelling natural SIRT3-directed lead, with mechanistic and animal evidence but no robust human target-engagement trial.
- NR has a reported direct allosteric SIRT5 activation effect in vitro; clinical significance remains unknown.
- MDL-800 and UBCS039 are research-only SIRT6 activators. Quercetin/isoquercetin and cyanidin are natural SIRT6 modulators with biochemical or structural evidence, not validated human anti-aging interventions.
- SIRT4 and SIRT7 currently lack convincing selective consumer activators.

## Editorial rule

Scores in the Atlas rank confidence in a claimed **sirtuin mechanism**. They are not clinical efficacy scores, longevity scores, dosing recommendations, or safety rankings. Sirtuin biology is tissue-, substrate-, disease-, and context-dependent; “more activity” is not universally beneficial.
