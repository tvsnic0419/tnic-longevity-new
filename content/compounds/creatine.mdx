> **TL;DR** — Creatine monohydrate is the most-validated ergogenic supplement in existence: decades of RCTs show it increases lean mass and strength when paired with resistance training. That muscle-preservation effect makes it a genuine **healthspan** lever against sarcopenia — Tier A for lean mass and strength, Tier B for cognition and longevity endpoints. 3–5 g/day, every day; no loading phase required.

## 1. What creatine does (and why it belongs in a longevity library)

Creatine is a small molecule your body already makes and stores mostly in muscle as **phosphocreatine** — a rechargeable battery for ATP. During short, high-demand efforts, creatine kinase transfers a phosphate from phosphocreatine to ADP to regenerate ATP almost instantly, buffering cellular energy faster than the mitochondria can. Supplementing raises muscle phosphocreatine stores, and paired with training that translates into measurably more strength and lean mass.

For a longevity audience the relevant endpoint isn't the gym — it's **sarcopenia**, the age-related loss of muscle that accelerates after 60 and predicts falls, frailty, and mortality. Muscle is also a metabolic and endocrine organ, so preserving it has reach beyond mobility. Creatine is one of the few supplements whose muscle benefit is Tier A rather than mechanistic hope.

**Primary hallmarks targeted:** [Mitochondrial dysfunction](/library/mitochondrial-dysfunction) (cellular energy buffering) · [Stem cell exhaustion](/library/stem-cell-exhaustion) (muscle satellite cells / sarcopenia) · [Altered intercellular communication](/library/altered-intercellular-communication) (brain energetics)

:::evidence tier="A"
The lean-mass and strength benefit of creatine plus resistance training is one of the most-replicated findings in sports-nutrition science, including meta-analyses in older adults where creatine augments the muscle and strength gains from training alone (Chilibeck et al., meta-analysis of resistance-training trials in aging). The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand (Kreider et al., *J Int Soc Sports Nutr*, 2017) summarizes the efficacy and long-term safety record. **Tier A for lean mass and strength; Tier B for cognition and direct longevity endpoints**, which are promising but not yet established at the same level.
:::

## 2. Mechanism

| Pathway | Mechanism | Hallmark link |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phosphocreatine energy shuttle | Regenerates ATP from ADP during high demand — a rapid cellular energy buffer | Mitochondrial dysfunction |
| Muscle protein / satellite cells | Supports training-driven hypertrophy and satellite-cell activity; counters sarcopenia | Stem cell exhaustion |
| Cell hydration signaling | Cell-volume increase is a mild anabolic and protective signal | Loss of proteostasis |
| Brain bioenergetics | Raises brain phosphocreatine; may aid cognition under stress/sleep loss | Communication |

:::practical title="When creatine is a near-automatic slot"
Creatine earns a slot for almost anyone doing resistance training, and especially **adults 50+ working to preserve muscle**. The benefit is amplified by training — creatine without a stimulus to adapt to does much less. Vegetarians and vegans often respond more strongly because their baseline muscle creatine is lower. The main honest caveat: it's a muscle-and-strength tool first; cognitive and longevity effects are promising but a tier below.
:::

## 3. Evidence summary

| Study type | Model | Key outcome | Tier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Creatine + resistance training | Human RCTs (incl. older adults) | Greater lean mass and strength vs training alone | A |
| ISSN position stand (Kreider et al. 2017) | Review of human data | Efficacy for performance + strong long-term safety record | A |
| Cognition (aging, sleep loss, vegetarians) | Human, emerging | Signals of benefit, not yet definitive | B |
| Longevity / healthspan endpoints | — | Muscle preservation is a plausible healthspan lever; no dedicated lifespan RCT | B |

**Consensus:** Tier A for lean mass and strength — the clearest human evidence of any compound in this library. Cognition and longevity framing are honest Tier B extrapolations from the muscle and bioenergetic data.

## 4. Dosing protocol

- **Dose:** 3–5 g/day of **creatine monohydrate**, every day (including rest days). Consistency matters more than timing.
- **Loading is optional:** a 20 g/day × 5–7 day load saturates stores faster but isn't necessary — daily 3–5 g reaches the same plateau in ~3–4 weeks.
- **Form:** plain micronized monohydrate. "Advanced" forms (HCl, buffered) cost more without proven advantage.
- **Take with fluids;** pair with resistance training for the actual benefit.

:::decision title="Should you take creatine?"
node | Do you do (or plan to do) any resistance training?
yes | Add 3–5 g/day monohydrate daily — this is one of the highest-evidence, lowest-cost additions available
no | node | Are you 50+ and focused on preserving muscle / preventing frailty?
  yes | Still worthwhile, but pair it with even light resistance work — creatine needs a training stimulus to shine
  no | It is safe, but the payoff is much smaller without a training stimulus
redflag | Diagnosed kidney disease → clear with your physician first; monitor with cystatin C, not creatinine (see below)
:::

## 5. Monitoring

Creatine supplementation raises **serum creatinine** slightly as a harmless assay artifact — it is not evidence of kidney damage in healthy people. If you need to track kidney function while supplementing, use **cystatin C**, which isn't affected.

| Biomarker / measure | Target | Frequency | Action if off-target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lean body mass | Trending up with training | Every 8–12 wk | Flat → check training stimulus and protein intake, not creatine dose |
| Grip / leg strength | Trending up | Every 8–12 wk | Flat at 12 wk → audit the training program |
| Cystatin C (if kidney concern) | Normal range | Baseline, then per physician | Rising → physician review (creatinine alone is misleading here) |

## 6. Safety, red flags, and contraindications

- **Exceptionally well-studied safety record** at 3–5 g/day over years in healthy adults.
- **Serum creatinine rises modestly** — an assay artifact, not kidney injury. Tell any clinician ordering labs that you supplement creatine.
- **Pre-existing kidney disease** — use only with physician oversight and cystatin C monitoring.
- **Hydration** — stay well hydrated, especially when training in heat.

:::redflag title="Coordinate before starting if any apply"
- **Diagnosed kidney disease** — physician oversight; monitor cystatin C, not creatinine
- **Pregnancy or breastfeeding** — insufficient supplemental-dose safety data
- **Interpreting labs** — always disclose creatine use so a raised creatinine isn't misread as renal decline
:::
