> **TL;DR** — Vitamin E is a lipid-phase antioxidant, but its supplementation story includes one of the more sobering findings in this library: the SELECT trial (Lippman et al., *JAMA*, 2009) found high-dose synthetic alpha-tocopherol (400 IU/day) modestly **increased** prostate cancer risk in the same large RCT that found selenium ineffective. Dietary vitamin E from food remains healthy; isolated high-dose alpha-tocopherol supplementation does not have a track record supporting broad use. Tier C; correct dietary gaps, don't megadose.

## 1. What vitamin E does — and the SELECT trial's uncomfortable finding

Vitamin E is a family of fat-soluble compounds (four tocopherols, four tocotrienols) that function as lipid-phase antioxidants, protecting cell membranes from oxidative damage and working alongside vitamin C (which regenerates oxidized vitamin E back to its active form). For decades, observational data linking vitamin E intake to lower cardiovascular and cancer risk fueled substantial supplementation enthusiasm. The **SELECT trial** (the same large RCT that corrected selenium's cancer-prevention reputation) delivered an even more striking finding for vitamin E: 400 IU/day of synthetic alpha-tocopherol was associated with a small but statistically significant **increase** in prostate cancer risk, leading to early termination of that trial arm.

**Primary hallmarks targeted:** [Mitochondrial dysfunction](/library/mitochondrial-dysfunction) (lipid-phase antioxidant) · [Chronic inflammation](/library/chronic-inflammation)

:::evidence tier="C"
SELECT (Lippman et al., *JAMA*, 2009, n≈35,533) found high-dose synthetic alpha-tocopherol (400 IU/day) increased prostate cancer risk versus placebo — a genuinely important negative finding from a large, well-designed RCT, not a fringe concern. Other large trials of isolated high-dose vitamin E have similarly failed to show cardiovascular benefit and, in some meta-analyses, showed a small increase in all-cause mortality at high doses. Dietary vitamin E intake (from food, as mixed tocopherols) remains associated with good health in observational data, but that's a different exposure than isolated high-dose supplementation. Tier C: isolated high-dose supplementation is not supported by the outcome-trial evidence, and carries a real signal of harm at high doses.
:::

:::practical title="Food, not a high-dose isolated capsule"
The honest read of this evidence base: get vitamin E from **food** (nuts, seeds, vegetable oils — naturally mixed tocopherols) rather than isolated high-dose alpha-tocopherol supplements. If deficiency is genuinely suspected (rare, usually related to fat-malabsorption conditions), that's a physician-guided correction, not a self-directed megadose.
:::

**Consensus:** Tier C. Isolated high-dose supplementation is not supported by outcome-trial evidence and carries a real signal of harm — a genuine example of "the food is fine, the megadose isn't."

## 4. Dosing protocol

- **Preferred:** dietary intake from nuts, seeds, and vegetable oils.
- **If supplementing:** stay at or below the RDA-level range (typically 15 mg/22 IU/day for adults) using a mixed-tocopherol product, not isolated high-dose alpha-tocopherol.
- **Avoid:** 400 IU/day or higher isolated alpha-tocopherol supplementation without a specific, physician-directed deficiency indication.

## 6. Safety, red flags, and contraindications

- **High-dose isolated supplementation** (400 IU+/day) has a documented cancer-risk signal (SELECT) and possible all-cause mortality signal in meta-analyses — this is the central safety concern for this compound.
- **Antiplatelet effect** — mild, more relevant at high doses; caution combining with anticoagulants.
- **Dietary intake** carries no comparable risk signal.

:::redflag title="Avoid high-dose isolated supplementation"
- **Currently taking 400 IU+/day isolated alpha-tocopherol** — reconsider given the SELECT trial finding
- **On anticoagulant/antiplatelet medication** — additional caution at any supplemental dose above dietary levels
:::
