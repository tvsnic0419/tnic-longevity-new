> **TL;DR** — Melatonin is a circadian-signaling hormone with genuine human evidence for reducing sleep-onset time — modest in magnitude, and most reliable at **low doses (0.5–3 mg)**, not the 5–10 mg typically sold. It's most clearly effective for circadian-rhythm problems (jet lag, shift work) rather than as a general sleep-quality booster. Tier B; also has an emerging, much-earlier-stage mitochondrial-antioxidant research thread.

## 1. What melatonin does — and the dose most products get wrong

Melatonin is a hormone secreted by the pineal gland in response to darkness, signaling the body's circadian clock rather than directly "causing" sleep the way a sedative does. Its best-evidenced use is **circadian realignment** — jet lag, shift-work sleep disruption, and delayed sleep-phase issues — where timed low-dose melatonin measurably shifts the sleep-wake cycle. For general insomnia, the effect on sleep-onset time is real but modest (typically minutes, not hours). A consistently reported and counterintuitive finding across the dose-response literature: **very high supplemental doses (5–10 mg, common on store shelves) are not more effective than low doses (0.5–3 mg) and may cause more grogginess and receptor-desensitization concerns.**

**Primary hallmarks targeted:** [Altered intercellular communication](/library/altered-intercellular-communication) (circadian signaling)

:::evidence tier="B"
Systematic reviews of melatonin for sleep-onset insomnia (referenced in American Academy of Sleep Medicine guidance) find a real but modest reduction in sleep-onset latency, with low-to-moderate certainty evidence. Circadian-realignment applications (jet lag, shift work) have a stronger and more specific evidence base for timing-based use. Dose-response studies generally do not show added benefit above 0.5–5 mg, and doses commonly sold (5-10 mg) exceed what the trial data supports as optimal. Tier B: real effect, most reliable for circadian use, at doses lower than typical commercial products.
:::

:::practical title="Lower dose, better timing — not more"
Use **0.5–3 mg**, taken 30–60 minutes before your target bedtime (or timed specifically for jet lag/shift-work protocols) — not a 10 mg dose "for good measure." More is not better here, and higher doses are more associated with next-day grogginess.
:::

**Consensus:** Tier B. Real, modest sleep-onset benefit; strongest and most specific evidence is for circadian realignment, at doses well below what's commonly sold.

## 4. Dosing protocol

- **General sleep-onset:** 0.5–3 mg, 30–60 minutes before bed.
- **Jet lag:** low dose (0.5–3 mg) timed to the destination bedtime, starting the day of travel.
- **Shift work:** timed to the intended sleep period, under a consistent schedule.
- **Avoid:** routinely using 5–10+ mg doses — the evidence doesn't support added benefit, and grogginess risk rises.

## 6. Safety, red flags, and contraindications

- **Well tolerated** at low doses; grogginess, vivid dreams, or headache are the main complaints, more common at higher doses.
- **Anticoagulant interaction** — some evidence of mild effects on clotting; coordinate if on blood thinners.
- **Immunosuppressant interaction** — melatonin has immune-modulating properties; caution if on immunosuppressive therapy.
- **Seizure disorders** — some evidence of altered seizure threshold; physician guidance advised.
- **Hormonal effects in children/adolescents** — a real consideration for that population, not addressed here (this module is written for adult use).

:::redflag title="Coordinate before starting if any apply"
- **On anticoagulants** — possible mild interaction
- **On immunosuppressive therapy** — melatonin has immune-modulating effects
- **Seizure disorder** — physician oversight advised
:::
