> **TL;DR** — Ashwagandha is an adaptogenic herb with real, replicated human RCT evidence for lowering cortisol and reducing subjective stress/anxiety — one of the better-evidenced adaptogens in this space. Standardized extracts (KSM-66, Sensoril) are what the positive trials used; loose/unstandardized root powder is a different, less-characterized product. Tier B; 300–600 mg/day standardized extract.

## 1. What ashwagandha does

Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) is a traditional Ayurvedic herb classified as an "adaptogen" — a substance proposed to help the body modulate its stress response rather than push it in one direction. Unlike many adaptogens with thin evidence, ashwagandha has a genuinely decent RCT base, mostly using **standardized root extracts** (KSM-66 and Sensoril are the two most-studied branded extracts) rather than raw powder, which matters because standardization controls for the withanolide content believed to drive its effects.

**Primary hallmarks targeted:** [Altered intercellular communication](/library/altered-intercellular-communication) (cortisol/HPA-axis) · [Chronic inflammation](/library/chronic-inflammation)

:::evidence tier="B"
Multiple placebo-controlled RCTs using standardized extracts (KSM-66, 300–600 mg/day) report significant reductions in serum cortisol and validated stress/anxiety questionnaire scores over 8–12 weeks. Smaller trials also report modest improvements in sleep quality and, in some male-specific studies, strength and testosterone measures. Effect sizes are real but moderate, and much of the positive literature comes from extract-manufacturer-funded trials — a real limitation to weigh, not a reason to dismiss the findings. Tier B.
:::

:::practical title="Standardized extract, not raw powder"
The positive trials used **standardized extracts** (KSM-66 or Sensoril) at controlled withanolide content. Raw ashwagandha root powder is a different, less-characterized product — if you want the evidence-backed effect, buy a standardized extract and check the label for the specific brand/standardization.
:::

## 3. Evidence summary

| Study type | Model | Key outcome | Tier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| KSM-66 RCTs (multiple) | Human, stressed adults | Reduced cortisol and anxiety/stress scores over 8-12 weeks | B |
| Sensoril RCTs | Human | Similar stress/cortisol findings with a different standardized extract | B |
| Sleep and strength/testosterone sub-studies | Human, smaller trials | Modest positive signals | C |

**Consensus:** Tier B. Genuine, replicated human RCT evidence for stress/cortisol with standardized extracts — one of the better-evidenced adaptogens, with the caveat of substantial manufacturer-funded trial representation.

## 4. Dosing protocol

- **Dose:** 300–600 mg/day of a standardized extract (KSM-66 or Sensoril), matching the trial doses.
- **Timing:** with food; some prefer evening dosing given the calming effect.
- **Duration:** 8–12 weeks to judge stress/cortisol effects.

:::decision title="Should you add ashwagandha?"

:::

## 6. Safety, red flags, and contraindications

- **Generally well tolerated** at studied doses; GI upset and drowsiness are the main complaints.
- **Thyroid effects** — can raise thyroid hormone levels; caution with hyperthyroidism or thyroid medication.
- **Immune-modulating** — theoretical concern in autoimmune conditions; caution advised.
- **Sedative interaction** — additive drowsiness with sedatives/alcohol.
- **Pregnancy** — avoid; traditional use includes abortifacient concerns not ruled out by modern safety data.

:::redflag title="Coordinate before starting if any apply"
- **Autoimmune thyroid disease (Hashimoto's, Graves')** — physician oversight given thyroid-hormone effects
- **Pregnancy or breastfeeding** — avoid
- **On sedative medication** — additive drowsiness risk
:::
