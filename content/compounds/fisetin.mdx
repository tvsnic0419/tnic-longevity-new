> **TL;DR** — Fisetin is a dietary flavonoid with the highest senolytic potency tested against senescent cells among flavonoids studied — eliminating 25–50% of senescent cells in vitro at physiological doses. Human clinical trials in older adults (Mayo Clinic's AFFIRM, NCT03430037) use an intermittent pulse protocol but have **not yet published** senescence-marker or physical-function outcomes. Tier B — strong preclinical and human-tissue data; human outcome data still pending.

## 1. Overview: senolytics and the SASP problem

Senescent ("zombie") cells stop dividing but don't die — instead they secrete a cocktail of inflammatory cytokines and proteases called the Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype (SASP), which damages surrounding healthy tissue. Senolytics are compounds that selectively clear these cells rather than merely suppressing the inflammation they cause.

**Primary hallmarks targeted:** Cellular senescence · Altered intercellular communication · Chronic inflammation

:::evidence tier="B"
Yousefzadeh et al. 2018 (PMID 30279143, *EBioMedicine*, Kirkland lab): fisetin eliminates senescent cells and extends health/lifespan in mouse models and is active in human tissue explants, identifying it as the most potent senolytic among flavonoids tested. Human clinical trials in older adults — Mayo Clinic's AFFIRM (NCT03430037) and further Phase 2 trials (NCT03675724, NCT04733534) — are ongoing; senescence-marker and physical-function outcomes are **not yet published**. Early reports indicate fisetin is well tolerated. Treat human efficacy as unproven pending trial readouts.
:::

## 2. Senolytic potency ranking vs quercetin, navitoclax

| Reported effect | Study | Hallmark link |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eliminates 25–50% of senescent cells in vitro | Kirkland lab, 2018 | Cellular senescence |
| Highest senolytic potency of flavonoids tested | Kirkland lab, 2018 | Cellular senescence |
| Reduced p16INK4A and p21 markers (aged mice) | Yousefzadeh 2018, mouse | Cellular senescence |
| Improved physical function (aged mice) | Preclinical, mouse | Altered intercellular communication |

Fisetin's selectivity comes from inhibiting BCL-2 and BCL-XL — anti-apoptotic proteins that senescent cells become uniquely dependent on for survival. Normal cells, which don't rely on that same survival signaling, are largely unaffected — this is the defining property that makes a compound a senolytic rather than a general cytotoxic agent.

:::practical title="Fisetin vs the dasatinib+quercetin protocol"
Quercetin is most studied in combination with the prescription drug dasatinib (the "D+Q" protocol). Fisetin has a standalone Mayo Clinic trial underway (AFFIRM) rather than a prescription-drug pairing — a meaningfully different evidence shape, though its human outcomes aren't published yet. Neither has completed a large longevity-endpoint RCT; see the full comparison for how the two evidence bases actually differ.
:::

## 4. Pulse-dose protocol: why 2-day burst, not daily

| Parameter | Recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| Dose | 100–500 mg fisetin |
| Protocol | Pulse: 2 consecutive days per month (matches the pulsed senolytic trial design) |
| Bioavailability | ~40% — liposomal or fat-based delivery preferred |
| Timing | AM |
| Why pulse, not daily | Senolytics are designed to clear existing senescent cells in a short window, not provide continuous background suppression — daily dosing has not been the studied protocol |

:::decision title="Should you run a fisetin pulse protocol?"
node | Do you have a scheduled surgery within the next 2 weeks?
yes | Do not start — fisetin may inhibit platelet aggregation at pulse doses
no | node | Are you on blood thinners or chemotherapy?
  yes | Consult a physician before any pulse protocol — real interaction risk
  no | Run the 2-consecutive-day pulse monthly, tracking inflammation markers and subjective function
redflag | Pregnant or nursing — avoid entirely; insufficient safety data
:::

## 5. Monitoring: p16/p21 proxies, inflammation markers

| Biomarker | Target | Frequency | Action if off-target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| hs-CRP | <1.0 mg/L | Baseline, each pulse cycle | Persistently high → review broader inflammation drivers, not just senescence burden |
| Subjective physical function | Improving trend | Monthly | No change after 3 pulse cycles → reassess whether senescence is your primary target |
| Platelet/bleeding signs | None | Ongoing during pulse days | Any unusual bruising or bleeding → discontinue and consult a physician |

## 6. Safety — low adverse event profile

:::redflag title="Pulse-specific risks — not a daily-supplement safety profile"
- **Poor bioavailability** — liposomal or fat-based delivery is preferred over standard capsules.
- **May inhibit platelet aggregation and CYP enzymes** at high pulse doses — real interaction risk with blood thinners and CYP-metabolized medications.
- **Avoid if scheduled surgery is within 2 weeks** — bleeding-risk precaution.
- **Avoid if pregnant or nursing** — insufficient safety data.
:::

- Consult a physician if taking warfarin or aspirin.
- Consult a physician if undergoing chemotherapy.
- Consult a physician for any CYP-metabolized medication before starting a pulse protocol.
