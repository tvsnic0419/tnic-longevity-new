> **TL;DR** — Fisetin is a flavonol that selectively triggers apoptosis in senescent ("zombie") cells by inhibiting the BCL-2/BCL-XL survival proteins those cells depend on. The evidence behind this is strong in mice (median and maximum lifespan extension) but genuinely limited in humans — the Mayo Clinic senolytic pilot trials that produced real human data used dasatinib+quercetin (D+Q), not fisetin alone. Take 500 mg on 2 consecutive days per month (pulsed, not daily); treat the senolytic claim as mechanistically compelling but not yet human-proven.

## 1. What fisetin does (and why it matters)

Fisetin belongs to a small class of compounds called **senolytics** — agents that selectively clear senescent cells rather than suppressing their downstream inflammatory output. Senescent cells stop dividing but don't die; instead they persist and secrete the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), a cocktail of inflammatory cytokines that accumulates with age and drives inflammation in surrounding tissue.

Fisetin's selectivity comes from a dependency senescent cells develop on anti-apoptotic proteins (BCL-2 and BCL-XL family members) to avoid triggering their own cell-death programs. Fisetin inhibits these proteins, which tips senescent cells — but not healthy ones — into apoptosis. This is the same "selective vulnerability" principle that defines the senolytic drug class broadly.

**Primary hallmark targeted:** Cellular senescence. **Secondary:** low-grade chronic inflammation, since fewer senescent cells means less SASP-driven signaling reaching neighboring tissue.

:::evidence tier="B"
Yousefzadeh et al., *EBioMedicine* 2018 (PMID 30279143): screening of 10 flavonoids identified fisetin as the most potent senolytic tested, selectively clearing senescent cells and reducing senescence markers across multiple tissues in mice; both acute and intermittent dosing extended median and maximum lifespan. This is a **mouse study** — it is preclinical evidence specific to fisetin. No dedicated human trial of fisetin alone has yet reported results confirming this senolytic effect in people.
:::

## 2. Mechanism — selective clearance of senescent cells

### 2a. BCL-2/BCL-XL-dependent apoptosis

| Step | Effect | Hallmark link |
| --- | --- | --- |
| BCL-2/BCL-XL inhibition | Removes senescent cells' anti-apoptotic shield | Cellular senescence |
| Selective apoptosis | Senescent cells die; healthy cells (not dependent on this survival pathway) are largely spared | Cellular senescence |
| SASP reduction | Fewer senescent cells secreting inflammatory cytokines | Chronic inflammation |
| Tissue-level effect (mouse) | Reduced senescence burden across multiple organ systems | Cellular senescence |

The selectivity is the entire point of a senolytic: fisetin doesn't broadly suppress cell survival signaling, it exploits a dependency that only senescent cells have developed. This is mechanistically distinct from anti-inflammatory compounds that only dampen SASP output without removing the cells producing it.

### 2b. Secondary antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties

As a flavonol, fisetin also has modest direct antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity independent of its senolytic action — consistent with the broader flavonoid class (comparable in kind, though not necessarily in magnitude, to compounds like quercetin and resveratrol). This secondary property is considered supportive, not fisetin's primary value proposition.

| Property | Plausible contribution | Strength of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Senolytic (BCL-2/BCL-XL) | Primary mechanism | Strong in mice; not yet confirmed in humans |
| Antioxidant | General flavonoid property | Mechanistically plausible; not the focus of the cited trial |
| Anti-inflammatory (SASP-independent) | General flavonoid property | Mechanistically plausible; not directly isolated in the cited trial |

:::practical title="When fisetin earns a stack slot"
Fisetin has clear value when **two or more** apply:

- Cellular senescence is a top-priority hallmark for you (age 50+, or elevated senescence-adjacent markers)
- You understand and accept that fisetin's human senolytic evidence is preclinical, not clinical
- You can commit to a pulsed protocol (not daily dosing) — this is how the mouse study and registered human trials are designed
- You are not expecting the same evidence tier as dasatinib+quercetin, which does have human pilot data

**Skip or defer:** if you're specifically looking for a *proven* human senolytic — that's currently D+Q under physician supervision, not fisetin alone; if you want a general anti-inflammatory/antioxidant flavonoid without the senolytic framing, resveratrol has more human data.
:::

## 3. Evidence summary

| Study | Design | Population | Key outcomes | Tier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Yousefzadeh 2018 (PMID 30279143) | Controlled feeding, screening of 10 flavonoids | Aged C57BL/6 mice, multiple senescence models | Most potent senolytic tested; cleared senescent cells, extended median/maximum lifespan | B |
| Mayo Clinic D+Q human pilots | Human pilot trials | Adults with age-related conditions | Reduced senescent cell burden and SASP markers — but this tested **dasatinib + quercetin**, not fisetin | — |
| Fisetin-specific human trials (registered, e.g. NCT03675724, NCT04733534) | Registered human RCTs | Older adults / age-related conditions | Registered; results not yet reported as of this writing | — |

**Consensus:** Tier B, and the tier reflects a real evidence gap, not a rounding error. The mouse data (Yousefzadeh 2018) is clean, was the strongest of 10 flavonoids screened, and shows genuine lifespan extension. But the human senolytic proof-of-concept that exists — Mayo Clinic's pilot trials — was built on dasatinib+quercetin, a different combination with a different mechanism profile than fisetin used alone. Registered trials testing fisetin specifically in humans exist but have not reported results. Read fisetin's Tier B rating as "outstanding preclinical signal, human confirmation still pending" rather than "proven senolytic."

**Fisetin vs D+Q:** these are not interchangeable claims. If you want the intervention with actual human pilot data behind it, that is D+Q, administered under physician supervision (dasatinib is a prescription tyrosine kinase inhibitor). Fisetin is the OTC option with the stronger preclinical mouse dataset but a currently thinner human evidence base.

## 4. Dosing protocol

| Parameter | Recommendation | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dose | 500 mg on 2 consecutive days per month (pulsed) | Mouse-dose-translated intermittent protocol; range used across sources is 100–500 mg |
| Form | Standard fisetin extract | Oral bioavailability is low (~40%); take with a fat-containing meal |
| Timing | AM with a fat-containing meal | Flavonoid absorption improves with dietary fat |
| Duration | Pulsed monthly (2 days on, ~28 days off) | Senolytic "hit-and-run" model — not a daily maintenance supplement |
| Cycling | Required by design | Intermittent dosing is how both the mouse study and registered human trials are structured; continuous daily dosing is not the studied protocol |

### Pulsed senolytic protocol schedule

| Month | Days | Time | Dose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Day 1–2 | AM with food | 500 mg |
| 1 | Day 3–30 | — | Off — no dosing |
| 2+ | Day 1–2 (repeat) | AM with food | 500 mg |

:::practical title="Titration and pulse-protocol checklist"
- [ ] Confirm you're using a pulsed (2 days/month) protocol, not daily dosing
- [ ] Take each dose with a fat-containing meal to support absorption
- [ ] Log any subjective changes (energy, joint stiffness) around each pulse, not just at random intervals
- [ ] Do not stack multiple senolytic-framed compounds in the same pulse window without spacing them out
- [ ] Revisit the decision to continue every 3–6 pulses rather than assuming indefinite benefit

## 6. Safety, red flags, and contraindications

- **Generally well tolerated** in the doses used in registered trials and typical OTC use
- **Mild GI upset** (nausea, stomach discomfort) reported occasionally at higher acute doses
- **Apoptosis-inducing mechanism** means the theoretical caution profile for senolytics broadly (see below) applies, even though fisetin's human safety data is limited

:::redflag title="Do not self-start without physician review"
- **Active cancer treatment** — apoptosis-inducing compounds warrant oncologist review before use, given potential interactions with treatment mechanisms
- **Recent major surgery or active wound healing** — senescent cells play a transient, functional role in tissue repair; timing senolytic use around surgery should be physician-guided
- **Anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications** — flavonoids can have mild antiplatelet activity; combine only with medical oversight
- **Pregnancy or breastfeeding** — no safety data; avoid
:::

:::warning title="Soft cautions"
Occasional mild GI discomfort (nausea, stomach upset) has been reported at higher acute doses; taking fisetin with food reduces this in most cases. Because human safety data on repeated fisetin pulsing is still limited compared to more heavily studied compounds, conservative dosing (staying at or below 500 mg per pulse) is reasonable until dedicated human trials report results.
:::
