> **TL;DR** — B12 is essential for red blood cell formation, nerve function, and (with folate) homocysteine metabolism. Deficiency becomes genuinely more common with age due to reduced stomach-acid production and absorption — and is a real, correctable cause of fatigue, cognitive symptoms, and elevated homocysteine that's frequently under-tested. Tier B; a correction compound with strong evidence for deficient/at-risk populations, especially those on metformin or acid-reducing medication.

## 1. What B12 does — and why deficiency risk rises with age

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is required for DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation, and myelin maintenance in the nervous system. Unlike most vitamins, B12 absorption depends on a multi-step process requiring adequate stomach acid and intrinsic factor — both of which decline with age (atrophic gastritis becomes more common), and both of which are directly impaired by two extremely common medications: **metformin** (documented to deplete B12 with long-term use) and **proton pump inhibitors/acid reducers**. That combination — rising absorption impairment plus common medication interference — makes B12 deficiency a genuinely under-recognized, easily correctable issue in older adults.

**Primary hallmarks targeted:** [Genomic instability](/library/genomic-instability) (DNA synthesis) · [Altered intercellular communication](/library/altered-intercellular-communication) (nerve function) · [Chronic inflammation](/library/chronic-inflammation) (homocysteine)

:::evidence tier="B"
B12 deficiency is a well-established, clinically validated cause of macrocytic anemia, peripheral neuropathy, cognitive symptoms, and elevated homocysteine — correction in deficient individuals reliably resolves these findings, which is Tier A-level clinical evidence for that specific population. Metformin's B12-depletion effect is documented in the literature this site already cites in its berberine-vs-metformin comparison. Broad supplementation benefit in B12-replete adults is much weaker. Tier B overall: strong deficiency-correction evidence, not a broad enhancement claim.
:::

## 2. Mechanism

| Pathway | Mechanism | Hallmark link |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DNA synthesis | Required cofactor for red blood cell and general cell division | Genomic instability |
| Homocysteine clearance | Cofactor for methionine synthase, converting homocysteine to methionine | Chronic inflammation |
| Myelin / nerve function | Required for maintaining the myelin sheath; deficiency causes peripheral neuropathy | Communication |

:::practical title="Test if you're on metformin, acid reducers, or are over 60"
These three groups have genuinely elevated deficiency risk: **metformin users** (documented depletion with long-term use), **chronic PPI/acid-reducer users** (impaired absorption), and **adults 60+** (age-related absorption decline). If any apply, a B12 level is a cheap, high-value test.
:::

## 3. Evidence summary

| Study type | Model | Key outcome | Tier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| B12-deficiency correction (anemia, neuropathy) | Human, clinical | Reliable resolution of deficiency-driven findings | A (for deficient individuals) |
| Metformin-associated B12 depletion | Human, observational + clinical | Documented, dose/duration-dependent depletion | B |
| Supplementation in B12-replete adults | Human | Weak evidence of added benefit | C |

**Consensus:** Tier B overall. Strong, near-definitive evidence for correcting real deficiency; weak evidence for supplementing an already-adequate level.

## 4. Dosing protocol

- **Test first:** serum B12, and consider methylmalonic acid (MMA) if borderline — MMA rises specifically with true B12 deficiency even when serum B12 looks marginal.
- **Correction (deficient):** 1000 mcg/day oral methylcobalamin for most cases; severe deficiency or absorption disorders may need physician-directed injections.
- **Maintenance (at-risk, e.g., on metformin):** 500–1000 mcg/day is a reasonable preventive dose.
- **Form:** methylcobalamin is the active, directly usable form; cyanocobalamin requires conversion but is also effective and cheaper.

:::decision title="Should you supplement B12?"
node | Are you on metformin, chronic acid reducers, or 60+?
yes | Test B12 (± MMA); supplement if low or borderline, then retest
no | node | Do you eat little to no animal products (vegan/strict vegetarian)?
  yes | Supplement — B12 is essentially absent from plant foods; 1000 mcg/day is reasonable
  no | Dietary intake is usually sufficient; test if any deficiency symptoms arise
redflag | Unexplained neuropathy or cognitive symptoms → get B12 and MMA tested before attributing symptoms elsewhere
:::

## 5. Monitoring

| Biomarker | Target | Frequency | Action if off-target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Serum B12 | Normal-to-high range | Baseline, 8-12 wk after starting | Still low → check absorption issues, consider injections |
| Methylmalonic acid (if borderline B12) | Normal range | With B12 if borderline | Elevated despite normal B12 → functional deficiency, treat |
| Homocysteine | <10-12 µmol/L | With B12/folate panel | Elevated → check both B12 and folate |

## 6. Safety, red flags, and contraindications

- **Extremely well tolerated** — B12 has no established toxic dose; excess is simply excreted.
- **Masking folate deficiency** — high-dose B12 can normalize blood counts in someone who actually has folate deficiency, delaying diagnosis; test both together when investigating anemia.
- **No significant drug interactions** at supplemental doses, aside from the depletion interactions noted above (metformin, acid reducers work against B12, not with it).

:::redflag title="Get tested, don't guess, if any apply"
- **Unexplained peripheral neuropathy or cognitive changes** — test before attributing to aging alone
- **Long-term metformin or PPI use** — periodic B12 testing is reasonable
:::
