> **TL;DR** — TMG (betaine) donates a methyl group to convert homocysteine back to methionine, reliably lowering elevated homocysteine — a real, well-established biochemical effect. The important caveat: large cardiovascular-outcome trials of *other* homocysteine-lowering agents (folic acid/B12/B6 in HOPE-2) lowered homocysteine without reducing cardiovascular events — so treat TMG as a genuine biomarker-lowering tool, not a proven event-reduction intervention. Tier B; 1–3 g/day.

## 1. What TMG does

Trimethylglycine (TMG, also called betaine) is a naturally occurring methyl donor — derived from choline and abundant in beets (the source of its common name) — that drives the **betaine-homocysteine methyltransferase (BHMT)** reaction, converting homocysteine back to methionine. This is a genuine, dose-responsive biochemical effect: TMG reliably lowers elevated plasma homocysteine, and it's used clinically at high doses to treat a rare genetic condition (homocystinuria) where homocysteine is severely elevated.

**Primary hallmarks targeted:** [Epigenetic alterations](/library/epigenetic-alterations) (methyl-donor / methylation) · [Chronic inflammation](/library/chronic-inflammation) · [Genomic instability](/library/genomic-instability)

:::evidence tier="B"
TMG's homocysteine-lowering effect is well established biochemically and is the basis for its approved clinical use in homocystinuria. For general cardiovascular risk reduction, the picture is more complicated: the large HOPE-2 trial (Lonn et al., *NEJM*, 2006) tested a different homocysteine-lowering combination (folic acid + B6 + B12) and found it lowered homocysteine without reducing cardiovascular events — an important, honest signal that lowering the *biomarker* doesn't automatically translate into lowering *events*. TMG's effect on the biomarker is Tier A-level reliable; its effect on hard longevity outcomes is unproven, hence Tier B overall.
:::

| Liver / lipid metabolism | Original clinical use was hepatic; some lipid-metabolism effects reported | Chronic inflammation |

:::practical title="Lower the number, but know what that does and doesn't prove"
TMG will reliably lower an elevated homocysteine level. What it has **not** been shown to do, based on the broader homocysteine-lowering trial literature (HOPE-2 and related trials with other agents), is definitively reduce cardiovascular events from that biomarker change alone. Use it to correct genuinely elevated homocysteine, understanding the outcome-trial gap.
:::

## 3. Evidence summary

| Study | Design | Key outcomes | Tier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| TMG homocysteine-lowering studies | Human, dose-response | Reliable, significant homocysteine reduction | A (for the biomarker) |
| HOPE-2 (Lonn et al., *NEJM* 2006) | Human RCT, different agents (folate/B6/B12) | Lowered homocysteine, did not reduce cardiovascular events | A (trial quality) / negative for events |
| Homocystinuria (clinical use) | Approved medical use | Effective at high doses for this specific genetic condition | A (for that indication) |

**Consensus:** Tier B for general longevity use. The biomarker effect is reliable; the outcome-level benefit is unproven by analogy to related trials.

## 4. Dosing protocol

- **Dose:** 1–3 g/day, typically split into two doses with meals.
- **Best evidence context:** for people with a measured, elevated homocysteine level (>10–12 µmol/L) rather than as a blanket addition.
- **Duration:** homocysteine changes are measurable within weeks; retest at 8–12 weeks.

:::decision title="Should you take TMG?"
node | Do you have a measured, elevated homocysteine level?
yes | 1–3 g/day TMG, retest homocysteine at 8–12 weeks
no | Lower priority — no clear rationale to lower an already-normal homocysteine
redflag | Existing kidney disease, or already on high-dose B-vitamin homocysteine-lowering therapy → coordinate with a physician to avoid over-treatment or masking other issues
:::

## 6. Safety, red flags, and contraindications

- **Well tolerated** at 1–3 g/day; GI upset (fishy odor in sweat/breath) can occur at higher doses in some people (trimethylaminuria-prone individuals).
- **Kidney disease** — coordinate with a physician; renal clearance considerations apply.
- **Don't skip the B12/folate check** — elevated homocysteine is very often a B12 or folate deficiency signal that should be corrected directly, not just masked by a methyl donor.

:::redflag title="Coordinate before starting if any apply"
- **Kidney disease** — physician oversight
- **Unexplained elevated homocysteine** — get B12 and folate checked first; deficiency is a common, directly correctable cause
:::
