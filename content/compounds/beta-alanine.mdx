> **TL;DR** — Beta-alanine raises muscle carnosine levels, which buffer the acid (H+) that accumulates during high-intensity exercise — one of the more mechanistically clean and consistently replicated sports-nutrition compounds. For a longevity audience, the relevant angle is the same as creatine: supporting the higher-intensity training that preserves muscle and cardiorespiratory fitness with age. Tier A for exercise performance; the one universal side effect (paresthesia — skin tingling) is harmless but worth knowing about in advance.

## 1. What beta-alanine does

Beta-alanine is the rate-limiting precursor for **carnosine**, a dipeptide stored in skeletal muscle that buffers hydrogen ions (acid) produced during high-intensity anaerobic exercise. Higher muscle carnosine means better buffering capacity, which translates to measurably improved performance in exercise lasting roughly 1–4 minutes at high intensity — the range where acid accumulation is the limiting factor. This is one of the more mechanistically clean and consistently replicated compounds in sports nutrition, with dozens of RCTs showing the carnosine-elevation and performance effects.

**Primary hallmarks targeted:** [Mitochondrial dysfunction](/library/mitochondrial-dysfunction) (exercise capacity) · [Stem cell exhaustion](/library/stem-cell-exhaustion) (muscle function/training capacity)

:::evidence tier="A"
Multiple RCTs and meta-analyses consistently show 4–6 weeks of beta-alanine supplementation (3.2–6.4 g/day) significantly raises muscle carnosine content in a dose- and duration-dependent manner, and improves performance in high-intensity exercise tasks lasting 1–4 minutes. This is a well-replicated, mechanistically well-understood effect — one of the more solid entries in the sports-nutrition literature. Tier A for the performance/carnosine-elevation claim.
:::

## 4. Dosing protocol

- **Dose:** 3.2–6.4 g/day, split into smaller doses (0.8–1.6 g at a time) to minimize paresthesia.
- **Duration:** 4–6 weeks minimum to build muscle carnosine to a meaningful level; benefits are cumulative, not acute.
- **Timing:** any time of day, split dosing; no need to time around workouts specifically.

:::decision title="Should you take beta-alanine?"
node | Do you regularly do high-intensity training (intervals, circuit training, sports with repeated sprints)?
yes | 3.2-6.4 g/day, split doses, for at least 4-6 weeks
no | Lower priority — the benefit is specific to high-intensity, 1-4 minute exercise tasks
redflag | None well-established at typical doses beyond the harmless paresthesia effect
:::

## 6. Safety, red flags, and contraindications

- **Paresthesia** (a harmless tingling/flushing sensation, usually in the face/neck/hands) is a well-known, dose-dependent side effect — not dangerous, but worth knowing about in advance so it isn't mistaken for an adverse reaction. Splitting doses reduces it.
- **Otherwise excellent safety record** at studied doses.
- **No significant drug interactions** established.

:::redflag title="Not a safety concern, but know this in advance"
- **Paresthesia (tingling)** is expected and harmless — split doses to minimize it, not a reason to stop
:::
