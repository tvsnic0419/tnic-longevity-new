> **TL;DR** — Selenium is an essential trace mineral that your body uses to build selenoenzymes — glutathione peroxidase and thioredoxin reductase — that neutralize peroxides and support thyroid hormone conversion. Correcting a genuine deficiency is clearly beneficial; adding selenium on top of an already-replete diet is not. The SELECT trial (35,533 men) found no cancer benefit, and both SELECT and a separate RCT (Stranges 2007) reported a diabetes signal. This is a narrow-therapeutic-window mineral: 55 µg/day covers the RDA; more is not better and can be harmful.

## 1. What selenium does (and why it matters)

Selenium is not a molecule your body burns for antioxidant effect the way it does vitamin C — it is a structural cofactor. It gets incorporated as the amino acid selenocysteine into roughly two dozen human selenoproteins, and it is those enzymes, not free selenium, that do the work. The two best-characterized families are the glutathione peroxidases (GPx), which reduce hydrogen peroxide and lipid hydroperoxides using glutathione as the electron donor, and the thioredoxin reductases (TrxR), which regenerate reduced thioredoxin and maintain redox balance. Iodothyronine deiodinases — also selenoproteins — convert thyroxine (T4) to active T3.

The key biological fact that shapes the entire evidence base: selenoprotein synthesis saturates. Once you have enough selenium to fully express GPx and TrxR, additional intake does not buy additional enzyme activity — it just raises the toxicity risk. That saturation is why "correct a deficiency" and "supplement a replete person" are two completely different interventions with opposite risk/benefit.

**Primary hallmarks targeted:** Genomic instability · Chronic inflammation

:::evidence tier="B"
The mechanistic role is not in doubt: selenoproteins including glutathione peroxidase and thioredoxin reductase are core redox enzymes whose activity is selenium-dependent (Handy & Loscalzo 2020, PMID 32380763, *Antioxidants* [Basel] 9(5):451). What is in doubt is whether supplementing beyond dietary sufficiency helps. The Selenium and Vitamin E Cancer Prevention Trial (SELECT) randomized 35,533 men and found that selenium "did not prevent prostate cancer" at a median 5.5 years (Lippman et al. 2009, PMID 19066370, *JAMA* 301(1):39–51). The trial was stopped early for futility, and its selenium arm contributed to concern about a diabetes signal.
:::

## 2. Mechanism — a cofactor with a ceiling, not a dose-response antioxidant

| Selenoprotein | Function | Hallmark link |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glutathione peroxidases (GPx1–4) | Reduce H₂O₂ and lipid hydroperoxides using glutathione | Genomic instability |
| Thioredoxin reductases (TrxR1–3) | Regenerate reduced thioredoxin; broad redox control | Chronic inflammation |
| Iodothyronine deiodinases (DIO1–3) | Convert T4 → active T3 | Altered intercellular communication (thyroid axis) |

The redox roles of GPx and TrxR are well-established biochemistry (PMID 32380763). But because these enzymes are the endpoint, the mineral behaves like a threshold nutrient, not a graded antioxidant: below sufficiency, adding selenium raises enzyme activity and measurably reduces oxidative damage; above sufficiency, enzyme activity is already maxed and extra selenium accumulates without benefit.

:::practical title="When selenium is worth your money"
Selenium earns a place in your stack in a **narrow** set of cases:

- Documented low selenium status (low dietary intake, low-selenium soil region, or a low plasma/serum selenium lab), **or**
- Diagnosed Hashimoto's/autoimmune thyroiditis, where selenium has modest antibody-lowering evidence (see Section 3).

**Skip it** if you eat Brazil nuts, seafood, eggs, or organ meat regularly, take a multivitamin containing selenium, or have no reason to suspect deficiency — you are likely already replete, and the trials say replete people get no upside and a possible diabetes downside.
:::

## 3. Evidence summary — clear deficiency correction, weak supplementation case

| Study | Design | N | Key outcome | Tier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lippman 2009 (SELECT), PMID 19066370 | RCT, 200 µg/d selenomethionine | 35,533 | No prostate/overall cancer benefit; stopped for futility | A (null) |
| Stranges 2007, PMID 17620655 | RCT (NPC secondary analysis), 200 µg/d | 1,202 | ↑ type 2 diabetes incidence, HR 1.55 (95% CI 1.03–2.33) | B |
| Vinceti 2018 meta-analysis, PMID 29974401 | Systematic review/meta-analysis | Multiple | Selenium "may increase the risk of type 2 diabetes" across exposure levels | B |
| Kong 2024 meta-analysis, PMID 38243784 | Meta-analysis of RCTs, Hashimoto's | Multiple RCTs | ↓ TSH and ↓ thyroid antibodies (TPOAb) with selenium | B |

**Consensus:** Tier B — honestly split. The mechanism is solid (Tier A biochemistry), and correcting deficiency is well-supported. But the flagship supplementation trials are null-to-harmful: SELECT found no cancer benefit in mostly selenium-replete American men, and both the Stranges RCT and the Vinceti meta-analysis point to increased type 2 diabetes risk. The one area with genuine supplementation upside is autoimmune thyroiditis, where meta-analysis shows lowered thyroid antibodies — but even there the clinical durability is debated.

:::decision title="Should you start selenium?"
node | Do you have a documented low selenium status or diagnosed autoimmune thyroiditis?
yes | Consider 55–100 µg/day (selenomethionine or selenium-enriched yeast) with food; retest status and, if thyroid-driven, TPOAb at 3–6 months
no | Do not supplement — a varied diet plus any multivitamin selenium already covers the RDA, and trials show no benefit past sufficiency
redflag | Already selenium-replete, diabetic/prediabetic, or planning high-dose (>200 µg/d) — the diabetes and selenosis signals apply directly to you; get physician clearance
:::

## 4. Dosing protocol

| Parameter | Recommendation | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RDA | 55 µg/day (adults) | The amount that fully supports selenoprotein activity in most people |
| Supplemental dose | 55–100 µg/day if repleting a deficiency | Do not chase higher — selenoprotein synthesis is already saturated near the RDA |
| Form | Selenomethionine or selenium-enriched yeast | Highly absorbed (~90%); the form used in SELECT and NPC trials |
| Timing | AM, with food | Fat-containing meal aids absorption |
| Hard ceiling | Stay well under the tolerable upper limit | The trials that showed harm used 200 µg/d long-term |

:::practical title="One Brazil nut ≈ your whole day"
A single Brazil nut can contain 68–90 µg of selenium — often more than the RDA. If you eat them regularly you may already be at or above target from food alone, which is exactly the "replete" scenario where the SELECT/Stranges data say added supplements stop helping and start carrying risk. Count dietary sources before adding a pill.
:::

| Biomarker | Target | Frequency | Action if off-target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Serum/plasma selenium | Mid-normal reference range | Baseline, 3–6 mo | Above range → stop supplementing; you are past sufficiency |
| Fasting glucose / HbA1c | Stable, no upward drift | Baseline, 6 mo | Rising → discontinue; the diabetes signal is the main supplementation risk |
| TPO antibodies (if thyroid indication) | Downward trend | Baseline, 3–6 mo | No movement → reassess whether selenium is doing anything for you |
| Selenosis signs (hair/nail brittleness, garlic breath, rash) | Absent | Ongoing self-check | Present → stop immediately and consult a physician |

## 6. Safety, red flags, and contraindications

- **This is a narrow-therapeutic-window mineral.** The gap between the RDA (55 µg) and doses associated with harm is small compared with most supplements.
- **Chronic excess causes selenosis** — hair and nail loss/brittleness, garlic-like breath, skin rash, GI upset, and neurological symptoms.

:::redflag title="Do not self-start high-dose selenium"
- **Type 2 diabetes signal** — a 200 µg/d RCT reported HR 1.55 for incident diabetes (PMID 17620655), and a meta-analysis found increased risk across exposure levels (PMID 29974401). Diabetic and prediabetic users should not supplement without physician oversight.
- **No benefit in the replete** — SELECT (PMID 19066370) found no cancer protection in a large, mostly selenium-sufficient population. Supplementing sufficiency adds risk without upside.
- **Selenosis / toxicity ceiling** — long-term high intake is toxic; respect the tolerable upper limit and count dietary sources (Brazil nuts especially).
- **Thyroid medication interplay** — if you have thyroid disease, coordinate with the physician managing your levothyroxine or antibody workup rather than self-dosing.
:::

- Consult a physician before combining selenium with any diabetes medication or if you have impaired glucose tolerance.
- Consult a physician if you are pregnant or nursing before exceeding the RDA.
