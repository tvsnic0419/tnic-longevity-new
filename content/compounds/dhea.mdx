> **TL;DR** — DHEA (dehydroepiandrosterone) is an adrenal hormone precursor that declines roughly 80% from young adulthood to age 70. Despite that steep, well-documented decline, restoring it in healthy older adults has **not** shown the broad anti-aging benefit the decline curve implies — the DHEAge study and similar RCTs found modest or mixed effects on body composition, well-being, and sexual function, not a robust healthspan signal. Tier C; a hormone that requires physician-guided use and monitoring, not a self-directed supplement.

## 1. What DHEA does — and why the decline curve oversold it

DHEA is produced by the adrenal glands and serves as a precursor hormone that peripheral tissues convert into both androgens and estrogens. Its blood level falls dramatically with age — roughly 80% lower at 70 than at 25 — which for years fueled the intuitive but ultimately unproven idea that "restoring youthful DHEA levels" would broadly restore youthful function. Placebo-controlled human RCTs testing that idea in healthy older adults, including the well-designed **DHEAge study** (Baulieu et al., *PNAS*, 2000), found modest and inconsistent effects — some improvements in specific subgroups (e.g., skin, some well-being measures in women) but no broad, robust restoration of vitality, cognition, or body composition across the general older population.

**Primary hallmarks targeted:** [Altered intercellular communication](/library/altered-intercellular-communication) (endocrine/hormone axis)

:::evidence tier="C"
The DHEAge study (Baulieu et al., *PNAS*, 2000) — a large, well-designed 1-year RCT in healthy older adults — found DHEA replacement produced only modest, inconsistent effects: some improvement in skin and libido measures, particularly in women, but no significant broad effect on strength, cognition, or general vitality. Other trials of DHEA for body composition and well-being show similarly mixed results. Tier C: a hormone with a genuinely dramatic age-related decline, but restoration has not shown the broad benefit that decline curve implies.

:::practical title="Test, and involve a physician — this is not a self-directed supplement"
DHEA is a genuine hormone with systemic effects (androgenic and estrogenic), sold over-the-counter in the US but functioning pharmacologically like a low-dose steroid precursor. Given the modest RCT-confirmed benefit and real hormonal-effect profile, this is a compound to discuss with a physician and use with lab monitoring — not a default addition based on the decline curve alone.
:::

## 4. Dosing protocol

- **Test first:** a DHEA-S blood level should inform any decision, ideally with physician guidance.
- **If pursued:** typical studied doses are 25–50 mg/day, lower for women given androgenic side-effect risk.
- **Physician involvement:** given the hormonal effects and modest evidence base, this should be a physician-guided decision with baseline and follow-up labs, not self-directed dosing.

## 6. Safety, red flags, and contraindications

- **Hormonal side effects** — acne, oily skin, and hair changes (androgenic) are real, dose-dependent effects, more prominent in women.
- **Hormone-sensitive cancers** — DHEA converts to androgens and estrogens; a real theoretical and biological concern in hormone-sensitive cancer history.
- **Liver function** — some evidence of effects on liver enzymes at higher doses; periodic monitoring reasonable with sustained use.
- **Drug interactions** — potential interactions with hormone therapies and some medications; physician review needed.

:::redflag title="Physician-directed use only — not a self-supplementation compound"
- **Hormone-sensitive cancer history** (breast, prostate, ovarian) — avoid without oncologist clearance
- **Any use should include baseline and follow-up DHEA-S and relevant hormone/lipid monitoring**
- **Pregnancy or breastfeeding** — avoid
:::
